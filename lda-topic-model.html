Latent Dirichlet Allocation,
explained and running live

LDA is a generative probabilistic model that discovers the hidden topics in a collection of documents. Every document is a mixture of topics, and every topic is a distribution over words. This page walks from the intuition to the maths, then trains a real model in your browser.

The intuition

Imagine a writer who, before typing a sentence, first picks a topic from their own personal blend, then picks a word that topic likes. LDA is the mathematical description of that writer, run backwards.

1

Documents are mixtures

A news article might be 60% politics, 30% economy, 10% sport. LDA represents each document as a vector of topic proportions θ that sums to one.

2

Topics are word distributions

The "economy" topic puts high probability on inflation, rates, bank, and near-zero on goalkeeper. Each topic is a vector φ over the whole vocabulary.

3

Words are the evidence

We only ever observe words. LDA infers which topic each word came from, and from those assignments reconstructs both the topics and the document mixtures.

Why "latent", why "Dirichlet", why "allocation"?

Latent because topics are hidden variables that are never observed directly. Dirichlet because the model places Dirichlet priors on both the per-document topic mixture and the per-topic word distribution. Allocation because each word token in the corpus is allocated to exactly one topic.

The generative model

LDA tells a story about how a corpus of D documents could have been written using K topics over a vocabulary of V words.

For each topic k = 1…K

Draw a word distribution φk ~ Dirichlet(β).

For each document d

Draw a topic mixture θd ~ Dirichlet(α).

For each word position n in document d

Draw a topic zd,n ~ Categorical(θd), then draw the word wd,n ~ Categorical(φzd,n).

Joint probability of everything p(φ, θ, z, w | α, β) = ∏k p(φk|β) · ∏d p(θd|α) ∏n p(zd,n|θd) p(wd,n|φzd,n)
α topic-mixture prior θ per-document topic mixture z topic of each word w observed word (shaded) φ per-topic word distribution β word-distribution prior

Why the Dirichlet?

A Dirichlet is a distribution over probability vectors. Its concentration parameter decides whether those vectors are spiky (few topics per document) or flat (many). Each dot below is one document's topic mixture over three topics, sampled live.

Small α → sparse documents

With α < 1, samples pile up in the corners of the simplex. Each document is dominated by one or two topics. This is what real text usually looks like, so α ≈ 50/K or smaller is a common default.

Small β → sharp topics

The same logic applies to topics over words. A small β (often 0.01) forces each topic to concentrate on a small set of words, which makes topics interpretable. Large β smears topics across the vocabulary.

Conjugacy is what makes inference tractable

The Dirichlet is the conjugate prior of the categorical distribution. That means after observing counts nk of each topic in a document, the posterior over θ is again a Dirichlet with parameters α + nk. This lets us integrate θ and φ out analytically, which is the trick behind collapsed Gibbs sampling below.

Inference: running the story backwards

The exact posterior p(θ, φ, z | w) is intractable. Two families of approximations dominate in practice.

MCMCUsed in the demo

Collapsed Gibbs sampling

Integrate out θ and φ, then repeatedly resample the topic of each word token given every other assignment. The update is beautifully simple:

Probability that token (d, n) belongs to topic k p(zd,n=k | z−(d,n), w) ∝ (nd,k + α) · (nk,w + β) / (nk + Vβ)

Left factor: how much document d already likes topic k. Right factor: how much topic k already likes word w. Words drift towards topics that are popular in their document and that already own similar words. Run for a few hundred sweeps and the assignments settle.

for sweep in range(iters):
  for d, n in tokens:
    k = z[d][n]
    n_dk[d][k] -= 1; n_kw[k][w] -= 1; n_k[k] -= 1
    p = (n_dk[d] + a) * (n_kw[:, w] + b) / (n_k + V*b)
    k = sample(p / p.sum())
    z[d][n] = k
    n_dk[d][k] += 1; n_kw[k][w] += 1; n_k[k] += 1
Optimisationscikit-learn, gensim

Variational Bayes

Instead of sampling, choose a simpler factorised distribution q(θ, φ, z) and tune its parameters to minimise the KL divergence to the true posterior. Equivalently, maximise the evidence lower bound:

ELBO log p(w) ≥ Eq[log p(w, z, θ, φ)] − Eq[log q(z, θ, φ)]

The 2010 online variational Bayes algorithm of Hoffman, Blei and Bach processes documents in mini-batches, which is why LDA scales to millions of documents and is the default in most libraries.

LDA in practice

What matters when you move from a toy demo to a real corpus.

Preprocessing decides everything

Lowercase, remove stopwords, drop tokens appearing in fewer than ~5 documents or more than ~50%, and lemmatise. Bigrams such as machine_learning often sharpen topics noticeably. LDA on raw text produces topics made of "the", "and", "of".

Choosing K

There is no single right answer. Sweep K and compare topic coherence (Cv or UMass), which correlates with human judgement far better than held-out perplexity does. Then read the topics. If two are indistinguishable, K is too high.

Evaluating topics

Use word-intrusion tests, coherence scores, and downstream task performance. Visualise with pyLDAvis to see topic overlap and term relevance. A topic that a domain expert can name in three words is a good topic.

Where it is used

Document clustering and navigation, recommendation (users as documents, items as words), customer-feedback mining, scientific-literature surveys, legal e-discovery, and as features for classifiers. It also underlies population-genetics models such as STRUCTURE.

Minimal Python

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.decomposition import LatentDirichletAllocation

vec = CountVectorizer(stop_words="english", min_df=2, max_df=0.5)
X = vec.fit_transform(docs)                       # docs: list[str]

lda = LatentDirichletAllocation(
    n_components=8, doc_topic_prior=0.1, topic_word_prior=0.01,
    learning_method="online", random_state=0)
theta = lda.fit_transform(X)                      # D × K document mixtures
phi = lda.components_ / lda.components_.sum(1, keepdims=True)  # K × V topics

terms = vec.get_feature_names_out()
for k, row in enumerate(phi):
    print(k, [terms[i] for i in row.argsort()[-10:][::-1]])

Common questions

Short answers to the things people ask after their first model.

Is LDA the same as Linear Discriminant Analysis?

No. They share an acronym and nothing else. Linear Discriminant Analysis is a supervised classification and dimensionality-reduction technique from classical statistics. Latent Dirichlet Allocation is an unsupervised generative model of text.

Why do I get different topics each run?

Both Gibbs sampling and variational inference start from random initialisations and converge to local optima. Fix a random seed for reproducibility, and run several seeds to check topic stability.

Does word order matter?

No. LDA is a bag-of-words model; it only sees counts. That is a limitation, but it is also why it is fast and why it works on any language once tokenised.

How much text do I need?

Thousands of documents of at least a paragraph each give reliable topics. Tweets and other very short texts are hard; consider pooling them by author or hashtag, or use a short-text variant such as Biterm Topic Model or BERTopic.

What are the classic extensions?

Correlated Topic Models (topics co-occur), Dynamic Topic Models (topics drift over time), Hierarchical Dirichlet Processes (K learned from data), Author-Topic and Supervised LDA (labels or metadata attached), and Labeled LDA (multi-label documents).
